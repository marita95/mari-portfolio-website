*This is a response to the first half of [this LessWrong post](https://www.lesswrong.com/posts/WRvxRHMCmc3Qt4axz/greater-good-arguments-in-ai-safety).*

They gave almost no good arguments about why EA philanthropy may be a bigger issue than most admit, and why leftists argue that it can further entrench societal and economic norms.

(1) I think it's a strawman, most lefties would disagree that deworming people or giving legal aid is negative/shouldn't be done nowadays, that's EV-adjacent thinking ("this is the most good I can do with my life") and only hardcore revolutionaries would agree with that probably (e.g. there are some very leftist folks who argue that reform is negative, because it allows people to get comfortable enough to put the revolution off). Black Panthers and other Marxist groups literally pioneered mutual aid, mutual aid is a huge huge thing on the left. It's top-down charity set by rich peoples' priorities that's the issue.

(2) Many people [in the US] DO like billionaires because they are propagandised by the idea of meritocracy; if they don't like them they have to contend with the idea that maybe being a good and smart person doesn't make you automatically rich. Also it is clearly useful to rich people to keep the poor people liking them, and if they can make poor people dependent on them they can justify their continued existence. I think Andrew Carnegie sticks out at me here... He was a philanthropist, but also believed in survival of the fittest and indirectly killed thousands of people + directly ruined lives and labour laws in the USA (one of the most consequential strikes was from his steel mill, the Homestead Strike). This is direct from his famous "Gospel of Wealth" (1889) which is well worth a read in its own right, but I think it's funny just how much this sounds like the exact talking points from EAs today:

> It is ever to be remembered that one of the chief obstacles which the philanthropist meets in his efforts to do real and permanent good in this world is the practice of indiscriminate giving; and the duty of the millionaire is to resolve to cease giving to objects that are not proved clearly to his satisfaction to be deserving. He must remember Mr. Rice's belief, that nine hundred and fifty out of every thousand dollars bestowed today upon so-called charity had better be thrown into the sea. As far as my experience of the wealthy extends, it is unnecessary to urge them to give of their superabundance in charity so called. Greater good for the race is to be achieved by inducing them to cease impulsive and injurious giving. As a rule, the sins of millionaires in this respect are not those of omission, but of commission, because they will not take time to think, and chiefly because it is much easier to give than to refuse. Those who have surplus wealth give millions every year which produce more evil than good, and which really retard the progress of the people, because most of the forms in vogue today for benefiting mankind only tend to spread among the poor a spirit of dependence upon alms, when what is essential for progress is that they should be inspired to depend upon their own exertions. The miser millionaire who hoards his wealth does less injury to society than the careless millionaire who squanders his unwisely, even if he does so under cover of the mantle of sacred charity. The man who gives to the individual beggar commits a grave offence, but there are many societies and institutions soliciting alms which it is none the less injurious to the community to aid.

^ This also hearkens back to eugenicist talking points from that era i.e. of the paupers who are weak of mind and heart and thus need paternalistic care from the rich men who know better. His point is that giving money to people can manufacture poverty, which is the same argument used today against welfare and "welfare queens".

(3) Speculative second-order effects being a problem of "optics" is kind of an understatement when we're talking about power and who wields it. Also, EA knows how to uses that power, bankrolling direct interventions and handling millions through Open Phil. It's additionally funny that they would mention this given longtermism's enormously speculative future predictions. Where's the burden of proof for that?

(4) Why are they assuming that they have higher understanding of economics than people who make those arguments? I'm not sure that's intrinsically the case. The history of leftist economic thought leads very strongly back to the early 19th century, it's not like it's an internet phenomenon that hasn't had any successes. Yes, you can point to communism having had enormous issues especially as related to forced collectivisation, but 1, capitalism has huge issues too and kills millions of people every year while contributing to the climate crisis, and 2, most people aren't asking for a new communist regime, but just... a more just society and maybe democratic socialist-adjacent (or even social democrat) measures for reducing impact on poorer people.

(5) Philanthropy has historically been used to launder reputations. I mean, hello??? Rhodes???? The Sackler family who manufactured the opioid crisis??? It's literally called ["reputation laundering"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reputation_laundering). Many EAs have arguments against this that I would be happy to explore in future, I know there are plenty of talking points that I have also seen on the EA forum but don't have the time to tackle at this moment.
